\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Steganography is the practice of hiding a secret message inside ordinary-looking content---an image, an audio clip, or a piece of text---in such a way that the existence of the communication is not apparent \cite{simmons1984prisoners,fridrich2009steganography}. When the carrier is text and the hiding method exploits natural-language structure rather than formatting, the practice is called \emph{linguistic steganography} \cite{fridrich2009steganography,zhang2020linguistic}. What makes this domain distinctly difficult is the low redundancy of language: there are simply very few harmless ways to alter wording without either shifting meaning or producing output that sounds wrong to a careful reader \cite{yang2020vae,kaptchuk2021meteor}. A single awkward synonym or implausible phrase is often enough to expose a hidden channel, which is why classical lexical and syntactic methods tend toward low payloads and leave statistical artifacts that modern steganalysis can reliably detect \cite{yi2022alisa,li2023rewriting,Wang_2023}.

Large language models (LLMs) have shifted this picture considerably. Their fluency, contextual sensitivity, and broad domain coverage have made it possible to build steganographic methods that rely on full text generation, paraphrasing, retrieval, and prompting---rather than local word substitution alone \cite{xi2025rise,brown2020language,wu2024generative,steinebach2024natural,lin2024zero,bubeck2023sparks}. The practical result is that the design space has broadened significantly since around 2022, with both open-weight and proprietary models appearing as generation backends \cite{xi2025rise,brown2020language}.

That broadening does not dissolve the underlying tensions. Pushing capacity tends to degrade fluency or statistical naturalness; optimizing for stealth may reduce throughput or robustness; balancing both without sacrificing deployability remains unsolved \cite{yang2020vae,kaptchuk2021meteor,ji2024principled,munyer2024deeptextmark}. Researchers have named this the Perceptual-Statistical Imperceptibility Conflict (PSIC) \cite{yang2020vae}. What the existing surveys have been slower to address is a related criterion: even text that is fluent and statistically clean may still be suspicious if it does not fit the surrounding discourse, task, or conversational setting. We call this \emph{contextual compatibility}, and it serves as the organizing lens for this review. Existing surveys and related reviews \cite{math9212829,Setiadi_Ghosal_Sahu_2025,Wang_2023} provide useful coverage of text steganography, but none centers contextual compatibility as a primary evaluative criterion for LLM-based methods.

We address that gap by synthesizing 32 primary studies on LLM-based linguistic steganography published between 2018 and 2025. Rather than treating generated text as acceptable whenever it is fluent or statistically plausible, we examine how closely it fits the surrounding discourse, task, and communicative context. The review maps the emerging design space, identifies recurring evaluation practices, and documents the main unresolved limitations.

Section~2 introduces background on steganography, evaluation criteria, and the role of LLMs in generative linguistic steganography. Section~3 reviews related surveys to position this study. Section~4 outlines the methodology. Section~5 presents findings across five research questions (RQ1--RQ5). Sections~6 and~7 discuss implications and conclude.
