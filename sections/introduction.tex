Steganography is the practice of concealing a secret message within ordinary-looking content---such as an image, an audio clip, or text---so that the existence of communication is not readily apparent. When the cover is text, the broader field is \emph{text steganography}, which also includes format-based techniques; when the method relies on natural-language content rather than formatting alone, it is known as \emph{linguistic} steganography. Large language models (LLMs) have recently become an important component in many such methods, which motivates the systematic synthesis in this paper.

In linguistic steganography, the available redundancy is limited: there are relatively few harmless ways to alter wording without changing meaning or sounding unnatural, which makes concealment fragile \cite{yang2020vae,kaptchuk2021meteor}. A single awkward synonym, statistically unusual phrase, or contextually implausible sentence can expose hidden communication. Classical approaches based on lexical or syntactic modification therefore tend to provide low payloads and leave detectable artifacts that modern steganalysis (automated detection of hidden data) can exploit \cite{Wang_2023}.

These models have changed this landscape by making high-quality text generation widely accessible. Their fluency, contextual sensitivity, and broad domain coverage have enabled a new generation of steganographic methods that rely on text generation, paraphrasing, retrieval, and prompting rather than only local word substitution.

These advances do not eliminate the core trade-offs of the field. Increasing embedding capacity can still degrade fluency, coherence, or statistical naturalness, while optimizing for stealth may reduce throughput or robustness. This tension is often described as the Perceptual-Statistical Imperceptibility Conflict (PSIC) \cite{yang2020vae}. Against this background, a systematic synthesis is needed to clarify how recent work defines the problem, evaluates success, and balances practical constraints. Several surveys and related reviews already discuss text or linguistic steganography \cite{math9212829,Setiadi_Ghosal_Sahu_2025}, but they do not foreground \emph{contextual compatibility} as a central criterion for assessing LLM-based methods.

This review addresses that gap by synthesizing recent primary studies on LLM-based linguistic steganography through the lens of contextual compatibility. Rather than treating generated text as acceptable if it is merely fluent or statistically plausible, we examine how closely it fits the surrounding discourse, task, and conversational setting. In doing so, the paper maps the emerging design space, highlights recurring evaluation practices, and identifies the main limitations that remain unresolved.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 introduces the background on steganography, its evaluation criteria and the capabilities of LLMs and their role in generative linguistic steganography. Section 3 reviews related surveys to position the present study. Section 4 outlines the review methodology, including the research questions, search strategy, and selection criteria. Section 5 presents the findings across five research questions (RQ1--RQ5). Section 6 discusses the implications and limitations of the review. Section 7 concludes the paper and outlines future research directions.
